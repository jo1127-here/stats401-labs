GEOSPATIAL VISUALIZATION

Mapping the Global Economy

One dataset. Two perspectives. Explore how color and area change our perception of 2025 nominal GDP.

01 / Dataset Description

The provided dataset contains the 50 largest economies by 2025 nominal GDP, measured in billions of current U.S. dollars. The data are based on the IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2025. Values may include estimates and can be revised.

GDP records are joined to geographic boundaries using ISO-3 identifiers. Countries outside the provided dataset are treated as missing data, not zero GDP.

03 / GDP Cartogram

Dorling cartogram: circle area represents GDP. Circle centers start near geographic locations and shift to reduce overlap. Country shapes and borders are replaced by circles.

Only economies with provided GDP are included. Circle area uses raw GDP regardless of the color-scale setting.

04 / Design and Comparison

The choropleth preserves country boundaries, relative locations, and geographic adjacency within an Equal Earth projection. Large territories such as Russia and Canada remain visually prominent because they occupy substantial map space, while high-GDP economies receive stronger colors. A logarithmic color scale distributes the highly skewed GDP values across the palette, making differences among smaller economies easier to see. The linear option exposes how strongly the largest economies dominate the range.

The Dorling cartogram replaces boundaries with circles whose areas are proportional to GDP. The United States and China become prominent economic masses, while geographically large economies with smaller GDP lose relative prominence. Compact economies such as Japan and Germany gain visual importance compared with their geographic footprints. Circle positions approximate geography, but displacement changes distances and adjacency; original shapes and borders disappear.

The choropleth best supports locating economies and identifying regional patterns. The cartogram better supports comparing economic magnitude and recognizing concentration among the included economies. Moving from color to area makes economic size occupy visible space rather than merely changing its shade. However, circle-area comparisons remain less precise than reading numerical values, and displaced countries can be difficult to identify. Linked highlighting, country selection, and shared tooltips preserve correspondence across the views. Gray countries on the choropleth and omitted circles indicate missing coverage, not zero economic output.
